# Part B — What did it cost?

## 4. Costing: three views of one route

Costing is where most local evaluations go wrong, and where care adds most value. The aim is not only to know what was spent, but to know what each route needed in order to work and what a commissioner would have to pay to keep it.

### 4.1 Six steps

1. Identify every resource the route uses: staff time, tests, consumables, drugs, premises, outreach, digital platforms, management and overheads.
2. Measure how much of each is used, ideally per person or per contact.
3. Value each resource using a unit cost, stating its source and price year.
4. Allocate shared costs using a stated rule, and test the rule.
5. Separate one-off set-up, evaluation and programme costs from recurrent delivery costs.
6. Assign each cost to the budget that pays it: council, NHS, funder or other.

### 4.2 Collect staff time from what people actually do

Staff time is usually the largest cost and the least well recorded. Job titles and budgets say what a post was funded to do, not what it did. Use session and rota records matched to activity logs for face-to-face time; short time diaries over two representative weeks to capture travel, administration and supervision; structured interviews with managers to apportion shared posts; and payroll or invoices to confirm the actual band and on-costs. Record non-contact time explicitly: a route that needs a lot of travel or follow-up will look cheaper than it is if only contact time is counted.

### 4.3 Top-down and bottom-up costing check each other

| | Top-down (gross) costing | Bottom-up (micro-) costing |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Divide a budget or spend line by activity | Count staff minutes, tests and supplies per person and multiply by unit costs |
| Strengths | Quick; captures everything paid for, including overheads | Shows what drives cost; transferable; separates fixed and variable costs |
| Weaknesses | Hides what drives cost; includes one-off spend unless separated | Time-consuming; easy to miss overheads and idle time |
| Use it for | What the funder bought; site totals | Cost per person at each stage; what would recur |

*Table 4.1. Top-down and bottom-up costing compared.*

### 4.4 Three cost views, never added together

A pilot carries costs that a mainstream service would not: design and co-production, recruitment and training, building digital tools, evaluation, programme management and early weeks of low activity. These belong in the funder's account of what it bought. They do not belong in the figure a commissioner uses to judge affordability. Report three cost views, side by side.

> **Note — Route X: three cost views.** The grant allocated £180,000 over 18 months, of which £170,000 was spent. Bottom-up costing finds that delivery consumed £90,000. At steady state, running Route X as a normal service would cost about £64,000 a year.

Two rules keep the three views honest. Hold one master figure for each site and cost view, and retire superseded figures openly in a short reconciliation, so that no reader meets two "totals" for the same thing. And annualise only over active months: Route X delivered for 15 of its 18 months, so its annualised delivery cost is £90,000 ÷ 15 × 12 = £72,000, not £60,000. The recurrent figure is lower again because a mature route runs fuller sessions.

> **Illustration from the BSWL evaluation.** Costs are reported in three views: top-down investment from the EJAF budget and reforecast, bottom-up delivery by route, and recurrent continuation. All three sites are costed on one basis — each site's own lines plus an equal share of project-wide costs — and superseded totals were retired in a reconciliation so that each site has one master figure. Costing ends where linkage ends: anonymous outreach supports a cost per contact, not a cost per PrEP start.

### 4.5 Shared costs need a stated rule

Shared costs, such as a project manager covering several routes or a clinic's premises, have to be allocated. State the rule, apply it consistently and show how the results change under a different one.

> **Important: Allocation rule.** Hold shared programme and provider overheads at site level. Allocate only direct costs, or a clearly labelled sensitivity share, to individual routes. An allocation is an analytical choice, not evidence that a funder paid separately for that route.

### 4.6 Retention drives cost per person

Starting someone on PrEP costs much more than seeing them again. In a Zimbabwe study an initiation visit cost $178 against $22 for a follow-up; because people stayed for only about three months, the cost per person-year on PrEP was $943, and keeping people for about five visits would more than halve it [9]. Route X's own unit costs show the same pattern.

> Cost per person-year on PrEP = (initiation cost + follow-up cost × number of follow-ups) ÷ person-years on PrEP

| Time on PrEP | Visits | Cost | Person-years | Cost per person-year |
|--------------|--------|-----:|-------------:|---------------------:|
| 3 months | Initiation only | £150 | 0.25 | £600 |
| 6 months | Initiation and 1 follow-up | £190 | 0.50 | £380 |
| 12 months | Initiation and 3 follow-ups | £270 | 1.00 | £270 |

*Table 4.3. Route X: initiation at £150 and three-monthly follow-up at £40 (illustrative).*

A route with a high cost per person started can still be good value if people stay on PrEP. A cheap route that loses people quickly may not be.

### 4.7 Keep the PrEP drug out of the council's figures

In England, councils pay for PrEP delivery from the Public Health Grant, and NHS England pays for the drug [10]. Generic tenofovir/emtricitabine bought through the national framework is cheap, but the price is confidential. Pharmacy reimbursement through the Drug Tariff runs to over £350 a month [11], which bears no relation to what the NHS pays. A council-perspective analysis should leave the drug out, or show it separately, and never price it at the Drug Tariff rate. For scale, delivering oral PrEP costs councils about £800 a year per person, against about £1,900 to £2,700 for injectable cabotegravir [10].

### 4.8 Where to find UK unit costs

Use the PSSRU and CHE York unit costs for staff time, with their inflation indices to bring older figures to one price year [12]; the NHS National Cost Collection for average NHS activity costs [13]; eMIT for generic medicine prices, not the Drug Tariff for PrEP [14]; and the Greater Manchester unit cost database for wider public-sector effects [15]. Local sexual health tariffs are prices, not costs, and serve only as a cross-check.
