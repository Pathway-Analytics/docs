## 7. Reading an ICER

An incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) answers one narrow question: for the extra money spent on this route compared with the alternative, how many extra people stayed on PrEP? It is the additional cost divided by the additional benefit — not the total cost, and not a verdict on its own.

- **Four possible results** — dominant (cheaper and better), dominated (dearer and worse), dearer and better (the usual case), cheaper and worse.
- **There is no threshold for "per person retained"** — NICE's per-QALY threshold does not apply; read an ICER against comparable routes, the comparator and a break-even reference point.
- **Break-even is a reference point, not a saving** — the direct value of a person-year on PrEP depends mainly on the HIV incidence in the group reached.

## 8. Savings and the wrong pocket

New services are often justified by savings elsewhere. Those claims need care: savings are usually smaller than expected, slower to arrive and in someone else's budget. Only cash-releasing savings reduce spending; channel shift is not a saving by default. The Provider Selection Regime lets value include benefits to taxpayers, provided they are labelled as such.

## 9. Uncertainty and evidence confidence

Every economic result is uncertain. The aim is to show how much, where it comes from, and whether it could change the decision — using proportionate methods, handling small numbers honestly, reporting evidence confidence separately from performance, and distinguishing a funder's share from its effect.

## 10. Judging value for money

Value for money is a judgement, not a number. The task is to make it transparent: what was weighed, against what standard, and why — the **five Es**, with standards agreed before the results are known, and evidence, judgement and decision kept apart.
