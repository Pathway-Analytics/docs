# Part D — Was it good value?

## 7. Reading an ICER

### 7.1 One narrow question

An incremental cost-effectiveness ratio answers one narrow question: for the extra money spent on this route compared with the alternative, how many extra people stayed on PrEP? It is the additional cost divided by the additional benefit. It is not the total cost of the route, and on its own it is not a verdict.

> ICER = (route cost − comparator cost) ÷ (people retained on the route − people retained under the comparator)

### 7.2 Route X against the clinic as usual

> **Note — Route X: the incremental comparison.** In the 18 months before the pilot, 20 women from the hubs' catchment started PrEP at the clinic; 15 started early enough to be followed for six months, and 9 were retained. At about £400 per start, the clinic's cost for that cohort was £6,000.

| | Clinic as usual | Route X | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Cost for the six-month cohort | £6,000 | £67,500 | £61,500 |
| People retained at six months | 9 | 45 | 36 |
| **ICER: cost per additional person retained** | | | **£1,708** |

*Table 7.1. Route X: incremental cost per additional person retained at six months (illustrative). Both sides exclude the PrEP drug.*

The ICER rests on the counterfactual: that without Route X, women in this population would have kept using the clinic at the baseline rate. The baseline is what makes that argument; without it, the ICER would be assumed rather than estimated. Report an ICER only when the extra outcome is positive and both sides of the comparison are credible.

### 7.3 Four possible results

- **Cheaper and better:** the route dominates. Adopt it, subject to affordability.
- **Dearer and worse:** the route is dominated. Do not adopt it.
- **Dearer and better:** the usual case, and Route X's. A judgement is needed on whether the extra outcome is worth the extra cost.
- **Cheaper and worse:** a judgement is needed on whether the saving justifies the loss.

### 7.4 There is no threshold for "per person retained"

NICE's threshold of £25,000 to £35,000 per QALY applies to its appraisals of medicines [27]. It is not a threshold for councils commissioning public health services, and no official threshold exists for cost per additional person retained on PrEP. Such a ratio is read in three ways: against other routes costed on the same basis, against the comparator, and against a break-even reference point.

### 7.5 Break-even is a reference point, not a saving

The direct value of a person-year of PrEP depends mainly on how likely that person was to acquire HIV without it.

> Break-even value per person-year on PrEP ≈ annual HIV incidence × PrEP effectiveness × lifetime care cost

A person retained at six months represents about half a person-year on PrEP, so Route X's ICER of £1,708 is roughly £3,400 per additional person-year. Treatment costs avoided would cover that only if incidence among the women reached were above about 2% a year at the higher lifetime cost, or above about 4% at the lower one. Neither is likely for this population. The conclusion stands: Route X's case rests on reach and equity, not on savings.

## 8. Savings and the wrong pocket

New services are often justified by the savings they will make elsewhere. Those claims need care: savings are usually smaller than expected, slower to arrive and in someone else's budget.

### 8.1 Only one kind of saving reduces spending

| Type | What it means | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|
| Cashable | Money that can actually be removed from a budget | A contract reduced because activity fell |
| Capacity release | Staff time or appointments freed; the budget does not fall | Clinic nurse time freed when routine follow-up moves online |
| Cost avoided | Future costs that will not arise, usually in another budget | Lifetime HIV treatment costs avoided by the NHS |

*Table 8.1. Three kinds of saving.*

> **Important: Savings rule.** Label every saving by type (cashable, capacity or cost avoided) and by the budget it falls to.

### 8.2 Channel shift is not a saving by default

Online STI testing roughly doubled testing uptake in a London trial [29], but a whole-system analysis found that it added tests, total cost and diagnoses rather than saving money [30]. Test any claim that a new route "frees up the clinic" by checking whether activity actually moved, or whether new demand was added on top.

> **Note — Route X: does it free up the clinic?** Route X was expected to reduce clinic PrEP appointments. Clinic PrEP attendances from the hubs' catchment did not fall during the pilot: the route added new users rather than moving existing ones. There is no capacity release to claim, which is consistent with the route reaching people the clinic was not.

### 8.3 Costs and benefits fall to different organisations

| Element | Who pays | Who benefits |
|---------|----------|--------------|
| Outreach, engagement and navigation | Council (or a charitable funder during a pilot) | People reached |
| PrEP consultation, tests and monitoring | Council, from the Public Health Grant | People on PrEP |
| PrEP drug | NHS England (arrangements from April 2027 to be confirmed) | People on PrEP |
| HIV treatment avoided | — | NHS |
| Health, work and social benefits | — | Individuals, families, employers and society |

*Table 8.2. Who pays and who benefits in PrEP provision in England.*

The Provider Selection Regime lets "value" include benefits to taxpayers through "reducing the cost burden of ill-health" [31], so NHS-side benefits can count in a council decision, provided they are labelled as such.

## 9. Uncertainty and evidence confidence

Every economic result is uncertain. The aim is not to remove uncertainty but to show how much there is, where it comes from and whether it could change the decision. It comes from parameters (unit costs, retention, incidence), from method (allocation rules, the comparator, the retention definition), from small numbers, from missing or unlinked data, and from attribution.

### 9.1 Use proportionate methods

| Method | What it shows | Use it for |
|--------|---------------|------------|
| One-way sensitivity analysis | How the result moves when one input changes | Unit costs, allocation shares, retention |
| Scenario analysis | Results under a coherent set of alternative assumptions | Pilot against steady-state costs; council against NHS perspective |
| Threshold (break-even) analysis | The value an input must reach for the conclusion to change | "How much would retention need to rise?" |
| Probabilistic sensitivity analysis | The combined effect of uncertainty in all inputs | Full models with distributions for inputs |
| Value of information | Whether more evidence is worth collecting | Usually qualitative locally: what would most change the decision? |

*Table 9.1. Proportionate ways of handling uncertainty.*

> **Note — Route X: how robust is the ICER?** Each scenario changes one input and leaves the rest at the base case.

| Scenario | ICER per additional person retained |
|----------|------------------------------------:|
| Base case | £1,708 |
| Clinic retained 12 rather than 9 | £1,864 |
| Delivery cost 10% higher (larger overhead share) | £1,896 |
| Stricter retention window: 40 rather than 45 retained | £1,984 |
| Measured at 90 days instead of six months | £1,325 (a different measure, not comparable) |

*Table 9.2. Route X: one-way sensitivity analysis (illustrative).*

The result is stable within about 15% across reasonable changes. The last row shows why endpoints must not be mixed: the 90-day ICER looks cheaper only because it measures something else.

### 9.2 Small numbers

Apply the data-sharing agreement's suppression rules, report ranges where denominators are small, and avoid percentages on very small bases. Nationally, only 589 Black African heterosexual women had an identified PrEP need in sexual health services in 2024 [17], so local counts in routes designed to reach them will be small.

### 9.3 Report evidence confidence separately from performance

A route can show a promising change with weak attribution, or a small effect with strong measurement, and the reader needs to know which. Decision makers increasingly read pilot evidence through Nesta's Standards of Evidence [32]: the HIV Action Plan for England 2025 to 2030 rated another EJAF-funded PrEP pilot at level 2 of 5 [23].

| Nesta level | In brief |
|-------------|----------|
| 1 | You can describe what the service does and why it matters |
| 2 | Data show some change among the people using the service |
| 3 | You can show the service is causing the impact, for example against a comparison group |
| 4 | You can explain why and how the service has the impact observed |
| 5 | The most advanced level: the impact can be replicated consistently |

*Table 9.3. Nesta Standards of Evidence, summarised.*

### 9.4 Attribution: a funder's share is not its effect

A local pilot cannot claim credit for a national trend; new HIV diagnoses in England fell in 2025 [33]. Funding intensity also changes how additionality should be read. A small grant supporting a mature service may enable a narrow innovation without causing all the activity around it. Full funding of a new route establishes where the inputs came from, but does not prove that every outcome was additional. Compare the plausible counterfactual, not the grant share.

> **Note — Route X: evidence confidence.** Route X has a baseline for the same population but no concurrent comparison group: Nesta level 2, approaching 3 if the baseline comparison holds up under scrutiny.

## 10. Judging value for money

Value for money is a judgement, not a number. The 2026 Green Book defines it as "the balanced judgement about the optimal use of public resources to achieve the objectives of a proposal" [6]. The task is to make that judgement transparent: what was weighed, against what standard, and why.

### 10.1 Five Es

UK aid evaluation uses four Es: economy, efficiency, effectiveness and equity [34, 35]. Oxford Policy Management's approach adds cost-effectiveness as a fifth [36], and we use all five:

- **Economy:** buying inputs of the right quality at the right price.
- **Efficiency:** turning inputs into outputs well, for example cost per person reached, started and retained.
- **Effectiveness:** achieving the intended outcomes, such as people starting and staying on PrEP.
- **Cost-effectiveness:** the extra outcome for each extra pound, compared with the alternative.
- **Equity:** reaching the people the service was meant for.

Whether a route can continue is judged separately, with two tests: affordable and sustainable (Module 11).

### 10.2 Agree the standards before the results

A rubric sets out, before any evidence is collected, what adequate, good and excellent value would look like against each criterion. Numbers and qualitative findings are then judged against those descriptions [36]. It suits pilots because it combines evidence that cannot be reduced to one ratio, openly.

A rubric is a reasoned judgement, not a scoring model. The 2026 Green Book recommends against multi-criteria analysis: simple weighting and scoring that "lacks an objective basis and can reduce transparency". Formal multi-criteria decision analysis, with swing weighting and an expert facilitator, is a different method, used only at the longlist stage of an options appraisal [6].

> **Important: Judgement rule.** Agree what adequate, good and excellent value look like with the funder and commissioners before the results are known, and publish the standards with the judgement.

### 10.3 Route X: the judgement

| Criterion | Evidence | Judgement |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| Economy | Nurse sessions at standard rates; venues provided free by the hubs | Good |
| Efficiency | £750 per start and £1,500 per person retained (delivery); recurrent £1,280 | Adequate, improving as the route matures |
| Effectiveness | 120 starts; 50% retained at six months | Good |
| Cost-effectiveness | £1,708 per additional person retained; savings unlikely to cover it | Adequate |
| Equity | 70% of starts from priority groups, against 15% at the clinic; lower retention in those groups | Excellent on reach; follow-up to strengthen |
| Affordable | £64,000 a year; £202,818 over three years, from the Public Health Grant | Conditional on the council's budget decision |
| Sustainable | Navigator post and booking licence funded only by the grant | Conditional |

*Table 10.1. Route X judged against an agreed rubric (illustrative). Continuation position: continue with conditions.*

### 10.4 Keep evidence, judgement and decision apart

Keeping them apart lets a commissioner see whether a weak result reflects a weak route or weak data, and whether a strong route is affordable to the body that would have to pay. The same evidence can then be translated for each audience without changing it.

| Question | Five Case Model | Five Es and continuation tests | Provider Selection Regime criterion |
|----------|-----------------|--------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| What did it cost? | Financial; economic | Economy, efficiency | Value |
| What did it achieve? | Strategic; economic | Effectiveness, equity | Quality and innovation; access and inequalities |
| Was it good value? | Economic | Cost-effectiveness; the overall judgement | Value |
| Can we afford to continue it? | Financial; commercial; management | Affordable and sustainable tests | Integration and sustainability; value |

*Table 10.2. One body of evidence, read through the frameworks each audience brings.*
