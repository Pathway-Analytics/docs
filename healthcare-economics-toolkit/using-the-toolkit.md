## 12. Rules of practice and reporting

### 12.1 Fifteen rules of practice

The rules set out in the modules, in one place.

| Rule | In one line | Module |
|------|-------------|:------:|
| Decision | Write down who decides what, by when and from which budget before choosing a method | 1 |
| Claims first | Define each claim, then the fields it needs; test them before go-live | 2 |
| Absence | Never record missing data as zero; say why it is absent | 2 |
| Reading | Every figure carries its cost view, period, denominator, comparator and evidence confidence | 3 |
| Three views | Report top-down, bottom-up and recurrent costs side by side; never add them | 4 |
| One figure | Hold one master figure per site and cost view; retire superseded figures openly | 4 |
| Annualisation | Annualise only over the months a route was actively delivering | 4 |
| Allocation | Hold shared costs at site level; allocate only direct costs or a labelled sensitivity share | 4 |
| Denominator | Cost per person retained only where linkage and retention are consistent; otherwise the last traceable stage | 5 |
| Endpoint | Name the retention endpoint; 90 days and six months are not interchangeable | 5 |
| Price year | Compare costs only in the same price year | 7 |
| Incidence | Never borrow HIV incidence from another population | 7 |
| Savings | Label every saving by type and by the budget it falls to | 8 |
| Judgement | Agree what good value looks like before the results are known | 10 |
| Continuation | Picking up a post is not the same as continuing the route it served | 11 |

*Table 12.1. Rules of practice.*

### 12.2 Ten questions to ask of any economic evaluation

A quick test for any study or business case, adapted from Drummond and colleagues [3].

1. Is the question clear, including whose perspective and which decision?
2. Are the options, including business as usual, fully described?
3. Is there credible evidence that the service achieves its outcomes?
4. Are all the important costs and consequences included?
5. Are they measured in sensible units?
6. Are they valued credibly, with sources and price years?
7. Are future costs and outcomes discounted where relevant?
8. Is the comparison incremental: extra cost against extra outcome?
9. Is uncertainty tested, and could it change the conclusion?
10. Does the report answer the questions the decision maker actually has?

### 12.3 Reporting standards

Report to CHEERS 2022 [46]; appraise models with the Philips checklist [47]; cost to the Global Health Cost Consortium Reference Case [48]; and design evaluations with HM Treasury's Magenta Book [49].

## 13. The evidence base for PrEP and sexual health

A summary of the economic evidence most relevant to commissioning PrEP and sexual health services in England, as at October 2026. It is a starting library, not a systematic review.

### 13.1 The UK economic case for PrEP is about clinic-attending men

| Study | Main result | Limits |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| Cambiano et al., Lancet Infectious Diseases 2018 [50] | Cost-saving over 40 to 80 years, after about 20 years of higher NHS costs; over 20 to 30 years, roughly £6,000 to £28,000 per QALY [51] | Gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men only; incidence about 2 per 100 person-years; 2017 drug prices |
| Ong et al., Eurosurveillance 2017 [52] | Cost-saving at 86% effectiveness; about £23,500 per QALY at 64% | Same population; no onward transmission |
| PrEP Impact Trial, Lancet HIV 2023 [20] | Incidence 86.8% lower among participants than among non-trial attendees | 96% gay and bisexual men; 333 cisgender women |

*Table 13.1. The UK studies behind the economic case for PrEP.*

> **Note — what "the case for PrEP is settled" does and does not mean.** UK modelling did not find PrEP cost-saving in every scenario. The main model found it cost-saving only over a 40- to 80-year horizon; over shorter horizons it was cost-effective rather than cost-saving [50, 51]. Both UK models concerned gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men attending clinics, at higher HIV incidence than today. The case is settled for that group at generic prices over the long term. It cannot simply be carried over to the people newer routes are designed to reach.

### 13.2 Cheaper drugs and lower incidence pull in opposite directions

Generic drug prices have fallen far below those assumed in 2017, which strengthens the case. HIV incidence has also fallen: NICE's committee for injectable cabotegravir adopted a baseline risk of 1.9 per 100 person-years, against estimates of about 0.95 from UKHSA and NICE's Decision Support Unit [19], and new diagnoses in England fell to 2,773 in 2024 [17]. Lower incidence means each person-year of PrEP averts fewer infections, which shifts the argument towards finding the people at highest remaining risk who are not yet reached.

### 13.3 Injectable PrEP

- **Cabotegravir:** NICE recommends it only for people who cannot take oral PrEP (TA1106, November 2025) [19].
- **Lenacapavir:** licensed by the MHRA in December 2025 [53]; not recommended in NICE's September 2026 draft guidance, with the committee meeting again on 3 November 2026 [54].
- International evidence finds injectables not cost-effective against generic oral PrEP at current prices [55, 56].
- Delivery cost to councils is higher: about £1,900 to £2,700 a year, against about £800 for oral PrEP [10].

### 13.4 How PrEP is delivered: what our review did and did not find

Our targeted review did not identify a UK or European economic evaluation of PrEP delivered through pharmacies, online services, general practice or nurse-led community models, or of PrEP for cisgender women or Black communities in high-income countries. It was not a systematic search, so such studies may exist. The most useful evidence we found comes from elsewhere: micro-costing that separates fixed from variable costs [9, 57]; US modelling that treats delivery improvements as interventions in their own right, in which helping people start PrEP produced most of the infections averted [58]; and analysis showing that outreach and navigation can make up most of a programme's cost, although most published studies leave them out [59]. An evaluation that costs engagement, navigation and linkage for new routes is adding evidence the literature lacks.

### 13.5 Teaching cases from English sexual health

| Case | What it teaches | Key finding |
|------|-----------------|-------------|
| Emergency department opt-out HIV testing [60, 61] | From activity data, to a cost-effectiveness model, to a funding decision | About £6 per test; NHS England committed £156 million for 2026 to 2029 |
| Online STI testing [29, 30] | Channel shift and whole-system cost | Uptake roughly doubled, but total cost rose |
| 4CMenB vaccine for gonorrhoea [62] | Price per dose as the decision variable | Cost-effective at £18 a dose; not at £85 |
| Publicly funded contraception [63] | Return on investment across horizons and budgets | £9 returned per £1 over ten years, mostly outside the NHS |
| Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis [64] | A recommendation made without economic evidence | Cost-effectiveness listed as a research gap |

*Table 13.2. Teaching cases.*

### 13.6 The value of an HIV infection averted

The figure most often used in the UK is a lifetime treatment cost of £360,800 undiscounted, or £185,200 discounted, per infection, from a 2015 study using 2013 prices and branded drugs; with generic drugs the discounted figure falls to about £101,200 [28]. We did not identify a more recent published UK estimate. State the price year, show the generic scenario, and use the figure as a reference point (section 7.5), not as a saving.

### A4. Example value-for-money rubric

| Criterion | Adequate | Good | Excellent |
|-----------|----------|------|-----------|
| Economy | Inputs bought at standard rates; costs recorded by route | Recurrent cost below that of comparable routes | Clearly lower, with shared resources used well |
| Efficiency | Cost per person started is known | Cost per person retained in line with comparable routes | Compares well, and falls as the route matures |
| Effectiveness | People are starting PrEP through the route | Most people who start are retained at follow-up | Retention matches or beats benchmarks for the group reached |
| Cost-effectiveness | An ICER is estimated, or the reason it cannot be is stated | In line with comparable routes | Compares well with comparable routes and the break-even reference point |
| Equity | Some people reached are from priority groups | Most are from priority groups | The gap between use and need measurably narrows |
| Affordable | Recurrent cost and payer identified | Fits the payer's budget over the planning period | Included in the payer's budget |
| Sustainable | Dependencies listed: staff, licences, data, owner | A costed route to continuation exists | Continuation agreed and built into a contract |

*Table A4. An example rubric. Agree the standards with the funder and commissioners before the results are known.*

### A5. Before you start: a checklist

- Decision, decision maker and date written down.
- Options and comparator agreed, including business as usual.
- Perspective chosen; other perspectives listed as scenarios.
- Claims listed; minimum viable dataset agreed, tiered by claim, with owners named and availability tested before go-live.
- Absence codes defined; data-sharing agreement and data protection impact assessment in place.
- Retention endpoint named (90 days, six months or both), with its follow-up window.
- Cost categories, allocation rules and unit-cost sources agreed; price year chosen.
- Equity breakdowns specified and suppression rules agreed.
- Value-for-money standards agreed with the funder and commissioners.
- Reporting dates aligned with budget-setting and contract decisions.

## Annex C. Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|------|---------------|
| Absence code | A label saying why a measure is missing: not applicable, not measured, not linkable, not yet mature or not defensible |
| Analytical lens | One of five views of the same evidence: core, signposting, bespoke, equity and value for money |
| Budget impact analysis | What a decision would cost a named budget, year by year |
| Cascade | The steps from being at risk to staying on PrEP: reached, assessed, started, retained |
| Cashable saving | Money that can actually be removed from a budget |
| Comparator | The option a new route is compared with, often the service as usual |
| Cost-consequence analysis | Setting costs alongside several outcomes without combining them into one number |
| Cost-effectiveness analysis | Comparing the extra cost of an option with the extra outcome, in natural units |
| Cost-utility analysis | Cost-effectiveness analysis where the outcome is the QALY |
| Counterfactual | What would have happened without the service |
| Discounting | Giving less weight to costs and benefits that arise in the future |
| Evidence confidence | How firmly a finding is supported, reported separately from performance |
| Five Es | Economy, efficiency, effectiveness, cost-effectiveness and equity |
| Funding intensity | A funder's share of a route's total resources; not its causal contribution |
| ICER | Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio: extra cost divided by extra outcome |
| Minimum viable dataset (MVD) | The common data specification an evaluation needs, set in proportion to its claims |
| Opportunity cost | The value of the best alternative given up |
| Perspective | Whose costs and benefits count in the analysis |
| PSR | Provider Selection Regime: the rules for arranging health care services in England |
| QALY | Quality-adjusted life year: one year in full health, or its equivalent |
| Recurrent continuation cost | What a route would cost each year to run as a normal service |
| Retention endpoint | The point at which retention is measured, such as 90 days or six months |
| Wrong pocket | When one organisation pays and another benefits |

*Table C1. Glossary.*

## Working with Pathway Analytics

Pathway Analytics is an independent strategy and public leadership consultancy, founded in 2009 and based in London. We help commissioners, providers, funders and boards decide what to fund, how to tell whether it works, and what it would take to keep it going.

A costing tool produces a unit cost; a standard economic model produces a cost per QALY. Neither, on its own, shows who was reached, what would recur or how the result enters a funding decision. Our work connects five things in one line of evidence:

- **Pathway design.** We cost the route a person actually takes, step by step, not a service average.
- **Real-world data.** We work with the records services already hold, agree a proportionate dataset before delivery starts, and say plainly where linkage stops.
- **Economics.** Cost views, comparators and ICERs, used only as far as the data support them.
- **Equity.** Who was reached sits in the main results, set against need.
- **Commissioning decisions.** Findings framed for budgets, business cases, council reports and procurement.
